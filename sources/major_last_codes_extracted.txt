                                          ABSTRACT


         This project explores the application and enhancement of the Elephant
         Herding  Optimization  (EHO)  algorithm,  aiming  to  improve  its
         effectiveness   in   solving   complex   optimization   problems.   By
         incorporating competitive, collaborative, and separtion mechanisms,
         Enhanced EHO achieves a superior balance between exploration and
         exploitation, enabling it to overcome limitations often encountered in
         standard  EHO  and  other  traditional  algorithms,  such  as  Genetic
         Algorithms (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Ant Colony
         Optimization  (ACO).  We  apply  Enhanced  EHO  to  a  variety  of
         benchmark  problems,  including  the  Traveling  Salesman  Problem
         (TSP), Rastrigin, Ackley, and the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP), as
         well as real-world applications in medical image enhancement and
         registration, specifically focusing on MRI scan clarity. Results indicate
         that Enhanced EHO outperforms other algorithms in solution quality,
         convergence speed, and robustness, particularly in multimodal, high-
         dimensional search spaces. The findings suggest that Enhanced EHO is
         a powerful tool for optimization across a range of applications and set
         the stage for future developments, such as adaptive parameter control
         and multi-objective optimization, to further improve its adaptability and
         performance in complex domains.

       1.1 INTRODUCTION TO OPTIMISATION ALGORITHMS


       Algorithms for optimization are important in computational science and engineering
       domains and facilitate developments in many areas such as AI, finance, engineering,
       logistics, healthcare, and so on. Optimization is the process of identifying a solution that
       best meets the targeted outcome to the level of an objective function subject to certain
       restrictions. In the optimization process, alternatives are sought in the most reliable,
       economical, or efficient manner from a large or small number of possibilities. The use of
       these methods is universal because they provide real-world solutions to mathematical
       models in the most efficient way possible, where time, financial or computer resources
       are often more variable in nature.


       Optimization goes hand in hand with what is called an objective function which basically
       represents the essence of what is being maximized or minimized in the optimization
       exercise. This may be a function of cost or a function of time or energy or any other metric
       that the goal can be expressed in terms of. The process to find the optimal solutions is not
       only about    defining this objective function but also about employing and defining the
       different constraints that can limit the area to search for the optimum. Constraints are
       those parameters which need to be met for a solution to be possible; such parameters can
       either  be  in  the  form  of  equalities  or  inequalities  and  are  the  reason  why  many
       optimization tasks are complicated. For example, in this case of logistics, a transportation
       cost optimization problem can try and achieve the minimum total transportation cost
       subject to parameters such as truck capacity, window and distance.


       Optimisation problems are generally classified as either linear or nonlinear. For linear
       optimisation, both the objective function and constraints are of linear origin and hence
       makes the models simpler and solvable by Simplex algorithm approaches. However, in
       practice, it is often the case that the underlying problems are non-linear leading to non-
       linear relationships between the variables which makes it more complex requiring more
       complex in nature solution processes like evolutionary algorithms or gradient descent.

       Nonlinear    optimization     is  a major    area   of  work   in  different   understanding      of  the
       underlying mathematics involved.


       Classes of Optimization Algorithms :


  1.   Gradient-Based Optimization: Gradient-based algorithms, such as gradient descent and
       its variants, are widely used for optimizing differentiable functions. In these methods, the
       gradient (or slope) of the objective function guides the direction of the search, aiming to
       move iteratively toward an optimal solution. Gradient               -based optimization is central to
       machine learning, especially in neural network training, where it adjusts weights to
       minimize a loss function. Advanced versions like stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and
       momentum-based  methods  (e.g.,  Adam)  offer  faster  convergence  and  increased
       robustness.


  2.   Metaheuristic Optimization: Metaheuristic algorithms are particularly useful for large,
       complex, and non       -convex problems where traditional methods struggle. Inspired by
       natural phenomena, these algorithms explore the solution space with techniques that
       combine exploration and exploitation. Notable metaheuristics include:



  o    Genetic Algorithms (GA): Inspired by the principles of natural selection and genetics,
       GA optimizes by simulating the processes of reproduction, mutation, and selection to
       evolve solutions over generations.


  o    Simulated Annealing (SA): Based on the annealing process in metallurgy, SA searches
       for solutions by gradually "cooling" the system, accepting not only better solutions but
       occasionally worse ones to escape local minima.



  o    Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO): Mimicking the behavior of bird flocks or fish
       schools, PSO optimizes by having a population of candidate solutions (particles) adjust
       their positions based on individual and collective experiences.


  o    Ant Colony Optimization (ACO): Modeled on the foraging behavior of ants, ACO uses
       a pheromone-trail metaphor to           guide the search for the best solution, often used for
       pathfinding and routing problems.

                                                           2

=== PAGE 13 ===
  o    Elephant Herding Optimization (EHO): Based on the social behaviors of elephant
       herds, EHO involves cooperation within groups (clans) and dispersal strategies, offering
       a balance of exploration and exploitation, and is effective for problems requiring dynamic
       adaptability.


  3. Deterministic  Algorithms:  Deterministic  optimization  algorithms,  like  the  Simplex
       method for linear programming, provide a predictable, exact solution given the same
       initial   conditions.   These   algorithms   follow   a   structured   approach,   relying   on
       mathematical models to converge to a solution. Though highly efficient for structured
       problems with a clear mathematical formulation, deterministic algorithms may be less
       effective for unstructured or highly nonlinear problems with many local minima.


  4. Derivative-Free Optimization (DFO): In cases where the objective function is non-
       differentiable  or  where  calculating  derivatives  is  computationally  expensive,  DFO
       methods are advantageous. These methods, such as the Nelder-Mead simplex method and
       Powell’s conjugate direction method, search for optima without relying on gradients,
       making them suitable for complex, noisy, or                real-world applications where function
       evaluations may be costly.



  5. Constrained Optimization Techniques: Many practical problems involve constraints,
       leading to the development of algorithms that specifically handle constraints, such as
       penalty methods, barrier methods, and Lagrange multipliers. Constrained optimization is
       critical in engineering applications, where solutions must satisfy physical, regulatory, or
       operational constraints. Specialized algorithms like Sequential Quadratic Programming
       (SQP) and Interior Point methods excel in handling these constraints effectively.


  6. Multi-Objective Optimization: Multi-objective optimization addresses problems with
       more  than  one  objective  function,  often  with  competing  goals.  Algorithms  in  this
       category, such as Pareto-based methods, aim to find a set of trade-off solutions rather than
       a single optimal solution. For example, in the design of a car, one might aim to minimize
       weight while maximizing safety, requiring a balance between objectives. Evolutionary
       algorithms like NSGA-II (Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II) and MOPSO
       (Multi-Objective Particle Swarm Optimization) are commonly used for such

       problems, enabling decision-makers to evaluate and choose from a set of Pareto-optimal
       solutions.


       The optimization as a field is also maturing as there are developed methods to deal with

       problems become more intricate. More attention is paid to hybrid algorithms that integrate
       the advantages of several optimization methods, particularly in a situation where neither
       deterministic nor heuristic method will work alone. Even machine learning is starting to

       In  the  last  years,  quantum  computing  can  be  considered  as  the  new  frontier  for

       1.2 NATURE INSPIRED OPTIMISATION: THE ORIGINS


       Numerous natural processes offer insights into how complex systems achieve balance,
       efficiency,  and  flexibility,  making  nature  a  long-standing  source  of  inspiration  for
       optimization algorithms. Nature is a prime example of optimization in dynamic, resource-
       constrained     environments,  from  the  complex  formations  in  physical  and  chemical
       systems to the evolutionary mechanics of genetics and animal social behaviors. Even with
       large  search   spaces    and   many    constraints,   nature's   amazing     ability  to  identify   near-

                                                            4

=== PAGE 15 ===
       optimal solutions is used by optimization algorithms based on these natural processes.
       Researchers have created techniques that not only address complicated issues but also
       adjust to the uncertainties and non-linearities frequently present in real-world situations
       by simulating these processes.



       Based on Darwinian evolution, Genetic Algorithms (GA) are among the first and most
       significant examples of nature-inspired optimization. A population of solutions in a
       genetic  algorithm  mimics  natural  selection  by  evolving  across  generations  through
       crossover,  mutation,  and  selection.  Genetic  algorithms  are  especially  useful  for
       navigating vast and intricate search spaces and locating answers to optimization issues
       that are difficult to resolve using traditional techniques, much like animals adapt and
       flourish                          in                          shifting                          habitats.


       The annealing process in metallurgy, when metals settle into their most stable condition
       through  regulated  cooling,  serves  as  the  model  for  another  popular  method  called
       Simulated Annealing (SA). By first permitting less ideal solutions (to avoid local optima),
       this  cooling  analogy  enables  simulated  annealing  to  find  the  best  answers,  then
       progressively honing the search as it "cools." Because of its ability to handle nonlinear
       optimization, simulated annealing has gained popularity in domains like engineering and
       logistics, where it might be difficult to identify a global minimum in a rough terrain of
       solutions.


       1.3  SWARM           INTELLIGENCE                :  COLLECTIVE              BEHAVIOURS               AS
       OPTIMISATION MODELS


       Nature is filled with examples of collective intelligence, where groups of organisms
       collaborate to accomplish tasks beyond the capabilities of individuals. Particle Swarm
       Optimization (PSO) and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) are two notable algorithms that
       draw from these behaviours.


       PSO is inspired by the movement patterns of bird flocks and fish schools, which rely on
       a balance of personal and collective knowledge to navigate toward a common goal.
       Similarly, in PSO, a group of particles adjusts its position based on both its own
       experience    and    that  of  its  neighbours.     This   approach     is effective    for  continuous

                                                           5

=== PAGE 16 ===
       optimization  problems  and  has  applications  in  neural  network  training,  resource
       allocation, and control systems.
       ACO,  on  the  other  hand,  is  based  on  the  foraging          behavior  of  ants,  which  leave
       pheromone  trails  to  mark  paths  to  resources.  Ants  follow  these  trails,  reinforcing
       successful routes and fading out less effective ones over time. This natural behavior is
       replicated in ACO algorithms, where agents search for optimal paths by favoring the most
       promising routes, making it highly suitable for routing, scheduling, and pathfinding
       problems. ACO demonstrates the power of decentralized decision-making and positive
       feedback, qualities that make it robust for combinatorial optimization problems, such as
       the Traveling Salesman Problem.


       1.4      RECENT              INSPIRATIONS                  :      MIMICKING                 SOCIAL
       STRUCTURES AND ENVIRONMENTAL SYSTEMS


       In recent years, the study of animal social structures has led to the development of
       algorithms like Elephant Herding Optimization (EHO) and Cuckoo Search. EHO, for
       example,  is  based  on  the  social  structure  and  behavior  of  elephant  herds,  where
       cooperation and dispersal play key roles in survival. In EHO, solutions are grouped into
       “clans” that work together and periodically introduce diversity by allowing certain
       solutions to leave and explore new areas of the search space. This approach balances
       exploration and exploitation and is especially effective for multi-modal and dynamic
       optimization tasks.


       Similarly, algorithms inspired by environmental processes, such as Firefly Algorithm and
       Water Cycle Algorithm, draw from the behaviors of natural elements. Firefly Algorithm,
       for instance, models the flashing behavior of fireflies to attract mates or             prey, using the
       concept of light intensity to guide particles towards promising solutions. The Water Cycle
       Algorithm mimics the hydrological cycle, where streams flow into rivers and oceans,
       representing the convergence of solutions. These methods reveal nature's strategies for
       achieving  stable  equilibrium  in  complex  systems,  guiding  algorithm  design  with
       principles of movement, attraction, and diffusion.


       Nature-inspired algorithms have proven versatile, resilient, and effective across diverse
       optimization challenges, from engineering design to machine learning. They offer a
       flexible framework for addressing real-world problems, where uncertainty, complexity,

       biology, chemistry, or physics—promises to further enrich optimization techniques,

       1.5 ENHANCED ELEPHANT HERDING OPTIMISATION


       Optimization problems, particularly in fields            like logistics, engineering, and medical
       imaging,   require   advanced   computational   methods   to   navigate   complex,   high-
       dimensional spaces with many local minima. Traditional algorithms, such as brute-force
       methods, often fail to provide efficient solutions within reasonable timeframes, especially
       as  the  scale  and  complexity  of  the  problem  grow.  Over  recent  years,  bio-inspired
       algorithms have demonstrated immense potential in addressing these challenges due to
       their ability to mimic the adaptive strategies observed in nature. Among these, Elephant
       Herding  Optimization  (EHO)  stands  out  for  its  ability  to  balance  exploration  and
       exploitation by emulating the social dynamics of elephant herds, making it highly
       effective for both classical and complex optimization problems.


       However, while EHO has been successfully applied to traditional optimization tasks such
       as the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) and Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP), its
       application in more advanced  and real-world problems,  particularly in the medical
       domain, still needs to be explored. This research investigates the efficacy of EHO across
       a  spectrum  of  optimization  problems,  from  well-established  benchmarks  to  novel
       applications in medical imaging. Specifically, this paper explores how EHO can enhance
       the quality of Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans and contribute to the generation
       of accurate 3D models, providing significant advancements in medical diagnostics and
       healthcare.


       Our project    explores   an  enhanced     form of   Elephant    Herding Optimization        (EHO)     by

                                                          7

=== PAGE 18 ===
       integrating  competitive,  collaborative,  and  separation  mechanisms  to  address  the
       limitations in traditional EHO models. While EHO is known for its ability to mimic the
       social dynamics and behaviors of elephant herds, including cooperation within clans and
       dispersion of individuals to explore the search space, our enhanced model introduces
       additional  layers  of  interaction  and  separation  strategies  that  significantly  improve
       optimization  performance,  particularly  in  complex,  multimodal  problems  like  the
       Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) and Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP).


       Traditional EHO focuses on maintaining a balance between exploration and exploitation
       through herd-based cooperation, where clans collectively search for optimal solutions
       while periodically introducing diversity. However, conventional EHO approaches can
       sometimes struggle with local optima and lack sufficient mechanisms to promote more
       diverse, adaptive exploration in dynamic search spaces. Our project addresses these
       challenges by introducing competitive mechanisms                 that rank individuals within clans
       based   on performance,       collaborative adjustments          that leverage information sharing
       among top-performing individuals, and separation strategies that periodically regroup
       clans based on their effectiveness in exploring new solutions. These mechanisms allow
       for a more structured, adaptive search process where the population dynamically adjusts
       according to competitive values while maintaining cooperative alignment within clans.


       The competitive mechanism is particularly innovative as it allows for selective pressure
       within clans, similar to evolutionary principles, where individuals compete for survival,
       enhancing  the  algorithm’s  adaptability.  Through             collaborative  mechanisms,  high-
       performing individuals influence their clan’s direction, sharing insights that enhance the
       search for solutions without converging too quickly. The separation strategy further
       enriches  the  model  by  regrouping  or  removing  certain  clan  members  based  on
       performance, enabling a continual refresh of the population and preventing premature
       convergence.


       Compared to existing EHO research, our approach goes beyond basic clan cooperation
       by adding multi-layered interaction and regulation methods. While existing EHO studies
       generally focus on a balance of herd behaviors, our enhanced model introduces elements
       similar to evolutionary operators found in genetic algorithms, such as selection and
       crossover, adapted specifically to the unique social structure of elephant herds. This
       hybrid approach allows our model to solve more intricate optimization problems with

  •  Exploration refers to the process of searching through new, uncharted areas of the solution
       space. It involves trying out different possibilities and exploring unknown regions to
       discover potentially better solutions.
  •  Exploitation refers to the process of refining and improving existing solutions based on the
       information already gathered. It involves focusing on known good areas of the solution
       space to enhance the current best solutions.


       Finding  a  balance  between  exploration  and  exploitation  is  crucial  in  optimization
       algorithms,  as  it  directly  impacts  the  algorithm's  ability  to  find  optimal  solutions
       effectively. Exploration involves searching new, often uncharted areas of the solution
       space to discover diverse solutions, which helps prevent premature convergence to local
       optima. Without adequate exploration, algorithms can become trapped in suboptimal
       regions, failing to locate the best possible solutions. On the other hand,                   exploitation
       focuses on refining existing solutions by intensively searching areas around the most
       promising  candidates.  Effective  exploitation  ensures  that  the  algorithm  fine-tunes
       solutions to reach the highest-quality results. A well-calibrated balance between these two
       components enables the algorithm to adapt dynamically, switching from exploration to
       exploitation  as  needed.  This  balance  is  especially  critical  in  complex,  multimodal
       landscapes, where the solution space is vast and contains numerous peaks and valleys.
       Achieving this equilibrium not only improves solution accuracy and convergence speed
       but also enhances the robustness of the algorithm, making it suitable for a wide range of
       real-world applications where solution landscapes are complex and evolving.

       def ackley(position):
