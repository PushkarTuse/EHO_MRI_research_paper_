Abstract
The sociological intricacies of nature motivate population-based optimisation through collective search and hierarchical influences. Elephant Herding Optimisation (EHO) represents these influences through clan organisation and leader-guided movement. This study examines an EHO-inspired formulation incorporating dimensionality-scaled attraction and iteration-dependent control parameters, alongside task-specific implementations for routing, multimodal optimisation and image processing. The evaluation considers the Travelling Salesman Problem, Vehicle Routing Problem, Rastrigin and Ackley functions, and synthetic rigid registration, with Genetic Algorithms, Ant Colony Optimisation and Particle Swarm Optimisation as comparators where available. The reported continuous-function results show task-dependent quality–time trade-offs: on Ackley, EHO records an objective of 4.80 compared with 5.09 for PSO, while taking 0.21845 s rather than 0.11082 s. Neither result reaches the known minimum. The imaging formulation extends the investigation to computational medical diagnostics through MRI intensity refinement, slice visualisation and isosurface-based three-dimensional reconstruction. Its reported comparison favours GA in PSNR and SSIM and EHO in processing time; the available measurement provenance does not support a validated MRI performance ranking. Routing feasibility defects and heterogeneous implementations likewise constrain attribution of the recorded outcomes to the proposed modifications. The study consequently identifies a candidate optimisation formulation and an exploratory MRI visualisation workflow, while distinguishing mathematical design, recorded numerical comparisons and demonstrated image-processing capability.
Keywords: Elephant Herding Optimisation; MRI enhancement; three-dimensional reconstruction; bio-inspired optimisation; computational medical imaging
1. Introduction
Population-based optimisation represents a search problem through interacting candidate solutions. The interaction rule determines how information about promising candidates is propagated and how alternatives remain accessible. Genetic Algorithms (GA) organise this process through selection, recombination and mutation [2]; Ant Colony System uses pheromone-guided construction of candidate tours [3]; and Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO) updates candidates using individual and collective search experience [4]. These mechanisms provide distinct ways of allocating search effort. Their biological or social analogies do not, by themselves, establish convergence rates or comparative performance.
Elephant Herding Optimisation (EHO) uses clan organisation, matriarch-guided updating and separation as its organising mechanisms [1]. Hierarchical influences are therefore part of its established foundation. The present formulation examines dimensionality-dependent scaling of attraction and iteration-dependent coefficients for attraction and repulsion. The scientific question is whether these changes yield a useful relationship between objective quality and computational effort. Distinguishing the proposed operators from the mechanisms inherited from EHO is necessary to define the contribution and to avoid attributing every favourable observation to the same modification.
The study connects this question to MRI processing, where a transformation of image intensities can alter both visible contrast and similarity to a reference. An optimiser must search over a specified representation using a defined objective; its name alone does not determine whether the output suppresses noise or preserves anatomy. Likewise, assembling slices into a volume and extracting a surface describe a visualisation procedure, whereas diagnostic assessment concerns a separate decision task. These distinctions guide the interpretation of the imaging component.
The investigation is organised around three forms of evidence. Routing examples examine the requirements imposed by discrete feasibility. Rastrigin and Ackley comparisons examine recorded objective trajectories and runtimes in continuous search. Synthetic registration and MRI visualisations examine image-processing formulations and their reported outputs. The implementations differ across these tasks, so the study treats them as related investigations rather than as a validated, integrated implementation of one algorithm.
2. Problem Statement
The optimisation problem is to reduce a task-specific objective subject to its feasible set and a finite computational budget. For TSP, feasibility is a permutation that visits each city once and returns to its starting point. For capacitated VRP, it includes customer coverage, route continuity and vehicle capacity. For the continuous test functions, candidates lie within coordinate bounds. Registration instead searches transformation parameters, while MRI enhancement searches intensity configurations. These representations impose different admissible update operations and prevent a continuous position rule from being transferred unchanged to a route encoding.
The central hypothesis is that controlling the scale and timing of EHO-inspired movement can improve the attainable objective for a given computational effort. Evidence for that hypothesis requires a comparison with the corresponding unmodified method as well as task-matched alternatives. A lower objective obtained from an infeasible route, a different evaluation budget or an unrelated implementation does not establish such an improvement. The present comparisons therefore distinguish recorded outcomes from a causal evaluation of the proposed modifications.
The imaging question concerns intensity processing and volumetric representation. PSNR, SSIM, registration MSE and processing time describe different endpoints and are interpreted according to their reference images, transformation models and measurement conditions. Tumour classification, diagnostic sensitivity, surgical accuracy and clinical outcomes are outside the evaluated scope. Computational medical diagnostics provides the application context; it is not an outcome inferred from image appearance.
3. Contributions
The work specifies dimensionality-scaled attraction and time-dependent control within an EHO-inspired search formulation, clarifying the roles of clan leaders, repulsion and diversity. It connects this formulation to discrete and continuous objectives while identifying the feasibility conditions needed for routing. It also presents the recorded comparisons with GA, ACO and PSO without imposing a universal ranking across solution quality and time.
The imaging contribution is a formulation of intensity refinement together with slice processing, volumetric assembly and surface visualisation. The accompanying analysis distinguishes synthetic registration from anatomical MRI, reference-image similarity from diagnostic accuracy, and surface appearance from geometric fidelity. The scope is exploratory: neither ablation of the proposed operators nor comparison with a learned imaging model is included in the reported results. Accordingly, the contribution is a specified direction for computational search and imaging analysis, rather than a demonstrated claim of algorithmic or clinical superiority.
4. Algorithmic Description of EHO
Elephant Herding Optimisation represents candidate solutions as members of interacting clans. Hierarchical influences provide a mechanism for directing local search, while additional variation operators alter the population beyond leader-directed interpolation. The framework below separates the proposed dimensionality scaling and parameter schedules from the task-specific implementations. This distinction is necessary because the benchmark programs use different update rules; their results do not constitute a controlled evaluation of one common modified EHO algorithm.
4.1 Elephant Herding Optimisation (EHO) Algorithm
Clan organisation and leader-guided search are inherited features of EHO [1]. The proposed changes concern the magnitude of attraction, a repulsive displacement from a poorly performing candidate, and iteration-dependent coefficients. These changes define candidate search operators rather than a new biological mechanism. All objectives in this section are minimised, so lower objective values indicate better candidates. The best and worst members of a clan are selected by the minimum and maximum of the same objective, respectively.

The continuous benchmark implementations divide the population with NumPy array_split. For C nonempty clans, their sizes differ by at most one: N mod C clans receive floor(N/C)+1 candidates and the others receive floor(N/C). A clan containing one candidate has no distinct follower; an update restricted to nonleaders then performs no search. Clan allocation is consequently part of the implemented algorithm, not an inconsequential storage choice.

5. Comparative Analysis of EHO with ACO PSO and GA
The comparisons examine task-specific implementations of Elephant Herding Optimisation (EHO), Genetic Algorithms (GA), Ant Colony Optimisation (ACO) and Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO). Solution quality and computational cost are distinct outcomes: a lower objective value does not imply a shorter runtime, and neither establishes a global optimum. The available results comprise individual observations and trajectories without a repeated-run estimate of uncertainty. Their interpretation is therefore restricted to the recorded configurations. The implementations also use different operators and computational budgets, so the algorithm names identify the variants considered here rather than interchangeable implementations of their canonical formulations.
5.1 Travelling Salesman Problem
The Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) seeks a minimum-length closed tour through N cities, visiting each city once. A candidate X=(X₁,…,XN) is a permutation of city identifiers, and d(Xᵢ,Xⱼ) denotes the distance between the corresponding cities. The objective includes each consecutive edge and the closing edge exactly once:
    (13)
The permutation representation determines which search operations are admissible. The supplied TSP variants use order crossover and swap mutation for GA and the herd-based method, a sequence of swaps for the PSO-labelled method, and pheromone-guided construction over unvisited cities for ACO. These discrete operators replace arithmetic updates on city identifiers. Minimising the tour length selects the best evaluated permutation; it does not certify that the tour is globally optimal.

5.2 Vehicle Routing Problem
The capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) assigns N−1 customers and a depot C₀ to at most M vehicle routes. Customer i has demand dᵢ and vehicle v has capacity Qv. A feasible assignment serves every non-depot customer exactly once, respects each vehicle capacity and begins and ends each nonempty route at the depot. Here D(Cᵢ,Cⱼ) is Euclidean distance, and an empty route has zero cost. These definitions distinguish the constrained routing problem from the evaluation of an arbitrary collection of customer sequences. The accompanying implementation computes the distance expression but does not enforce complete customer coverage, uniqueness or capacity during scoring. Its initial route construction respects accumulated capacity locally, yet can leave customers unassigned. Subsequent EHO updates retain a random subset of each route and supplement it from the herd leader; uniqueness is checked only within that route. The PSO-labelled update adds a numerical velocity to customer identifiers and applies modulo arithmetic. This can insert the depot or duplicate and omit customers. Neither procedure supplies the feasibility-preserving routing adaptation claimed by a constrained VRP solver.
5.2.1 Observations and Analysis
The reported example uses 20 non-depot customers and five vehicles. Its source configuration sets vehicle capacity to 50, uses 30 candidates for each method and runs 100 iterations; the EHO population is divided into five herds. The recorded EHO cost is 523.85, whereas PSO is recorded as 693.38 in the numerical account and 693.39 in the plotted label. This last-digit discrepancy does not change the principal limitation: lower distance can arise from omitted service, and the recorded candidates lack a feasibility certificate. Accordingly, these costs describe the unconstrained scoring output rather than a demonstrated reduction in feasible routing cost.

6. Benchmarking EHO on Complex Functions
Continuous benchmarks separate objective reduction from the additional feasibility requirements of routing. Rastrigin and Ackley supply explicit multimodal objectives with a known minimum of zero. Their source configurations use ten decision variables; these experiments therefore concern ten-dimensional search, rather than a demonstrated scaling law for increasing dimensionality. The reported trajectories and endpoint values are analysed with respect to their objective definitions and implemented operators.

    (16)
The global minimum is f(0)=0. Consequently, each positive value in the comparison is also its absolute objective gap from the known minimum. The source configuration uses n=10, populations of 50, five EHO herds and 1000 iterations.
6.1.1 Implementation of Elephant Herding Optimisation
The implemented herd-based search initialises candidates uniformly within the coordinate bounds. Each candidate proposes a random convex combination of its current position and the best position in its herd; that proposal is accepted when it improves the objective. A subsequent mutation occurs with probability 0.2 and adds a vector drawn uniformly from the coordinate domain, followed by clipping to the bounds. This probability is constant in the supplied Rastrigin implementation. Mutation may worsen the current candidate, while a separate best-observed objective is retained. This implementation therefore differs from a time-decaying mutation schedule, and the trajectory alone does not identify the contribution of dimensionality scaling or the adaptive parameters introduced in the general framework.
6.1.2 Comparative Analysis with Particle Swarm Optimisation
The PSO comparator combines inertia with displacement toward personal and global best positions. The trajectory-producing source variant uses inertia 0.99 and cognitive and social coefficients of 0.2, whereas an earlier source variant uses 0.95 and 0.3. These distinct configurations prevent the algorithm label alone from specifying the experiment. Although both comparison loops use 1000 iterations, EHO can evaluate a candidate repeatedly during acceptance and mutation, so equal loop counts do not imply equal objective-evaluation budgets. The observed difference is not an estimate of the performance of every PSO or EHO configuration.

7. EHO for Synthetic Image Registration
Registration extends the optimisation comparison to image-derived objectives. The experiment estimates rotation and translation between a generated reference array and its transformed counterpart. Its images are random intensity fields; the resulting evidence concerns synthetic registration and remains distinct from the MRI enhancement and reconstruction application.
7.1 Definition of Image Registration and Its Challenges
Registration estimates a spatial mapping that relates a moving image to a reference image. The transformation class and discrepancy measure are separate modelling choices. The present three-parameter model includes one rotation and two translations, with no scaling or deformable field. An intensity discrepancy measures agreement after resampling; it does not independently establish recovery of anatomical correspondences.
7.2 The Impact of Random Noise on Image Registration
A random field is not an anatomical image contaminated by a measured acquisition process. In the synthetic test, the reference is a 100×100 array of uniformly distributed intensities in [0,1), generated with NumPy seed 42. The moving array is obtained by applying a 25° rotation and translations of 5 and −3 pixels. This construction provides a controlled transformation task but contains no tissue structures or patient variation. Additive noise, motion and interpolation artifacts therefore require distinct interpretations rather than a single claim of robustness to medical-image noise.

MSE is computed over the complete array without an overlap mask. 7.5 Framework for Applying EHO in Image Registration
Each candidate contains an angle bounded by [−45°,45°] and two translations bounded by [−20,20] pixels. The active EHO-labelled implementation forms a random convex combination of the current candidate and its herd leader, then adds 0.005 times the displacement toward the global best. Bounds are enforced by clipping. The population contains 30 candidates in five herds, and the search is capped at 20 iterations. This operator has no scaling parameter, dimensionality exponent or adaptive mutation schedule; the synthetic registration result therefore does not test all components of the general modified-EHO formulation.
7.5.1 Adaptations for Noise-Induced Challenges
The implemented discrepancy is a direct MSE evaluation of the transformed array. There is no averaging of repeated noisy evaluations, explicit smoothing of the fitness function or separate noise-removal stage in this registration experiment. Candidate displacement changes the proposed transformation rather than smoothing image intensities or objective values. Its effect on noise tolerance cannot be separated from search behaviour by the available single-field comparison.
7.5.2 Implementation of Comparative Algorithms
The PSO comparator uses inertia-weighted velocity updates with inertia 0.7 and cognitive and social weights of 1.5. The GA uses random parent sampling, single-point crossover and independent parameter mutation with probability 0.1; its parent selection is not tournament selection. The ACO-labelled comparator shifts continuous coordinates using a shared pheromone vector, with evaporation 0.1 and deposits inversely related to the best MSE. It does not construct graph-based paths. Each comparator uses 30 candidates and 50 iterations or generations, in contrast to the EHO cap of 20. These are custom baseline implementations rather than learned registration models.

7.6.3 Scalability Testing
The workload experiment processes batches of 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 generated pairs sequentially. Because image generation resets seed 42 on every call, all reference arrays are identical and their fixed transformed counterparts repeat as well. Figure 6 therefore measures repeated-work processing cost, not generalisation across a growing imaging cohort. Its EHO endpoint at 50 pairs is approximately 27–28 s by visual reading, and the GA endpoint is approximately 67 s; these plotted readings do not support the alternative 17 s and 60 s values. Exact endpoint reconstruction is unavailable from the aggregate records. No parallel execution path is used, and output quality is not recorded as a function of batch size.

7.7 Overall Insights
The synthetic comparison describes a non-dominating relationship between image discrepancy and runtime. PSO attains the lowest recorded MSE, ACO the shortest recorded time, and EHO lies between these endpoints. Search budgets and custom baseline operators limit causal interpretation, while the repeated-input workload supplies no evidence about variation across anatomy. The results characterise these implementations without establishing a general ranking of registration algorithms.
7.8 Applications and Implications
Training-free parameter search offers a way to formulate image processing without fitting a predictive model to training cases. Its practical value nevertheless depends on per-image computation, image quality and the requirements of the intended workflow. No learned-model comparison, resource-cost analysis or clinical outcome measurement is included here. The subsequent MRI study concerns slice enhancement and volumetric visualisation; the synthetic registration example is a separate optimisation experiment rather than a validated stage of an integrated diagnostic pipeline.
8. EHO for MRI Image Enhancement and 3D Reconstruction
MRI processing provides an application context for hierarchical population search in computational medical diagnostics. The formulation considered here separates intensity refinement, slice display, volumetric assembly and surface extraction. These stages have different outputs: enhancement changes image appearance, whereas reconstruction represents the spatial relationships encoded in an acquired volume. Their contributions are therefore considered separately.
8.1 Role of MRI Enhancement in Diagnostics and 3D Modeling
The enhancement problem is to modify the intensity representation while retaining information needed to interpret anatomical structure. Contrast adjustment, noise suppression and structure preservation are distinct criteria; improving one does not establish improvement in the others. Population-based optimisation offers a way to search over candidate intensity configurations, but the resulting image quality depends on the objective and constraints as well as the search rule. The present analysis concerns image processing and visualisation rather than a measured diagnostic endpoint.
8.1.1 Need for 3D Reconstruction in Comprehensive Analysis
A slice displays a cross-section of a spatially indexed volume. Combining slices in acquisition order makes their three-dimensional relationships accessible in a single view without adding anatomical information beyond the acquisition. Geometry depends on slice position, orientation and voxel spacing. Consequently, an intensity change and an improvement in geometric accuracy are different outcomes, and interpolation or smoothing alone cannot establish anatomical fidelity.
8.1.2 Overview of the BRATS Dataset and Its Relevance
BraTS provides the intended multimodal MRI and tumour-annotation context for the imaging application. The results presented here are restricted to a slice labelled 71 and selected surface illustrations; they do not define an identifiable BraTS release or patient cohort. Tumour annotations and clean intensity references also serve different purposes: a segmentation label does not supply the noise-free image required to evaluate denoising fidelity.

The proposed intensity-refinement formulation represents a candidate as an image-intensity matrix. Its intended criteria are contrast adjustment, noise suppression and preservation of structure. A complete scalar objective combining these criteria is not specified, so the update equations describe candidate-search mechanisms rather than a fully determined restoration algorithm. Combining slice inspection with a volumetric display can organise anatomical information for visual analysis. In this study, that role is confined to representation of the processed images. 8.6.2 Computational Scope and Broader Applications
For computational medical diagnostics, the relevant operational trade-off is image quality relative to the resources consumed by the complete processing chain. Enhancement, volume assembly and rendering contribute different costs. The reported slice times cover an unspecified processing boundary and cannot be converted into hospital throughput or compute savings. Likewise, the formulation contains no completed learned-model comparison or validated transfer to CT, PET or SPECT. Its scope remains exploratory MRI intensity processing and visualisation.
9. Conclusion
This study relates EHO-inspired search to routing, multimodal optimisation and MRI visualisation through explicit distinctions between candidate representation, objective function and evaluation endpoint. Dimensionality-scaled attraction and time-dependent control coefficients define the proposed extension; clan hierarchy remains an inherited mechanism. The mathematical formulation explains how movement is regulated, but does not establish global convergence or identify the causal contribution of an individual modification.
The recorded numerical comparisons do not support a uniform ranking. EHO attains a lower reported Ackley objective than the selected PSO configuration at a longer runtime, and the Rastrigin trace includes a prolonged plateau before further improvement. Routing outcomes are constrained by feasibility defects in the available implementations. Synthetic registration measures intensity agreement between generated images, whereas the MRI component provides an intensity-processing formulation and qualitative surface visualisations. Its reported quality and timing values lack the measurement provenance needed for a validated empirical comparison. These distinctions bound the conclusions of the study.
The practical implication is that optimiser selection in computational medical imaging must consider image quality and computational effort together, with a task-appropriate reference and a valid feasible set. The present work supports investigation of hierarchical search for MRI processing; it does not establish diagnostic accuracy, real-time readiness or superiority over learned methods.
10. Future Directions
The next empirical question is whether the proposed operators improve a fixed, reproducible baseline under matched evaluation budgets. Original EHO, dimensionality scaling, scheduled attraction, repulsion and their combined formulation require separate comparisons. Routing evaluation must enforce customer coverage, uniqueness and capacity before reporting objective values. Repeated random seeds should be analysed separately from variation across problem instances or patients, using mean and standard deviation, uncertainty intervals, paired effect sizes and prespecified statistical tests. Convergence should be reported against objective evaluations and elapsed time rather than iteration count alone.
For MRI, evaluation requires identified cases and modalities, spatial metadata, a defined enhancement objective and justified image-quality references. Patient-level separation between tuning and testing is necessary when several slices originate from one volume. Anatomical registration and geometric reconstruction fidelity require their own endpoints; image similarity and smooth surfaces cannot substitute for them. Noise sensitivity, parameter sensitivity and failure cases are also pertinent to the intended processing task.
A comparison with learned methods can then address accuracy, training requirements, per-case optimisation or inference time, and memory use on stated hardware. Training and checkpoint preparation should be separated from recurring processing cost. A hybrid method constitutes a separate hypothesis and requires optimiser-only, learned-only and combined comparisons. GPU execution and cloud processing remain implementation possibilities whose value depends on measured end-to-end latency, resource consumption and workload. Broader datasets can test generalisation; clinical adoption would additionally require evidence concerning a defined workflow and its users.
